# Environment extraction

Turn a source photo's setting into a few editorial relief-print cues. Preserve atmosphere and place memory without rebuilding the room or reducing negative space to wallpaper.

## Build the environment lock

1. Inspect the source beyond the pet. List only clearly visible elements; do not guess location or object identity.
2. Rank candidates by recognizability, emotional relevance, silhouette simplicity, and compatibility with the selected paper field.
3. Choose one **primary anchor** and zero to two **micro-accents**. Prefer elements near the pet or elements that explain the emotional beat.
4. Record: `ENVIRONMENT LOCK — primary: [one cue]; accents: [zero to two cues]; treatment: [fragment/band/shadow/texture]; coverage: [percentage]; omit: [clutter].`
5. Keep the lock through prompt compilation and retries.

## Selection rules

- Favor simple cues: a floor seam, rug check, doorway edge, low cabinet rhythm, window-light block, curb line, puddle reflection, grass tuft, blanket fold, or the silhouette of one familiar toy.
- Convert complex furniture and rooms into one or two incomplete planes, bands, or carved rhythms. Never reproduce the full photographic background.
- Preserve a specific accessory or toy only when it contributes to the story or is visually distinctive; otherwise treat it as clutter.
- Ignore people, hands, feet, screens, logos, readable text, cables, containers, pet-like reflections or printed animals, and accidental partial animals unless explicitly requested. Preserve every clearly posed primary pet from the source unless the user selects a subset.
- Do not invent an outdoor scene from an indoor source or add decorative nature motifs without evidence.

## Density and hierarchy

- Default environment coverage: 8%–15% of the canvas; allow up to 18% only when the setting is important.
- Preserve at least 55%–65% one-piece coherent negative space after environment cues are added.
- Keep the primary anchor behind, beneath, or offset from the dog. Never place a high-contrast shape directly behind the face.
- Use fewer and lighter carved marks than the dog. Environmental line density should stay around one-third to one-half of the dog's medium-detail zone.
- Let at least half of every environmental form dissolve into paper. Avoid complete rectangles, closed rooms, horizon-to-horizon floor bands, and full-width shadows.
- At thumbnail size, the dog must remain the first read and the environment the second or third read.

## Color behavior

- Keep the paper contrast lock authoritative. Do not change the paper to match the source room.
- Reuse the dog's two-to-four ink palette. Allow at most one quiet environmental accent already present in the source.
- Reduce source colors to low-saturation blocks. Do not reproduce every color from a patterned rug, blanket, or room.
- When an environment cue touches the dog, make it lighter, cooler/warmer in the opposite direction, or more broken than the adjacent coat.

## Placement patterns

- **Ground memory:** two or three short floor seams, rug checks, curb cuts, or grass strokes directly beneath and beside the paws.
- **Offset fragment:** one incomplete cabinet, doorway, window, or furniture edge occupying a corner away from the face.
- **Quiet echo:** repeat one source shape two or three times at decreasing density, such as tile lines, blanket folds, rain cuts, or shelf slats.
- **Object whisper:** retain one small toy or accessory silhouette at low contrast and no more than one-third of the dog's head size.

## Failure correction

If the page becomes busy, remove accents before shrinking the dog. If the environment competes with identity, reduce its contrast and coverage before darkening the dog. If the source has no meaningful cue, use only a tiny grounding rhythm and keep the rest blank.
